# Chapter 13: Turnip Lanterns and Iron Pipes

Thursday arrived with a sky the color of galvanized tin and a frost that didn't melt when the sun came up.

Across the valley, the puddles in the gravel ruts had frozen into milky, opaque lenses of ice. The old iron water pump behind the orphanage barn was completely seized; Michael had to thaw the leather valve with a boiling kettle of water before the kitchen could draw enough for Mrs. Gable’s morning wash-up.

By second period, the Advanced Physics classroom at Oakhaven High was so cold that Mr. Harris hadn't even bothered taking off his thick brown tweed overcoat. He sat behind his oak desk with his wool scarf tucked into his lapels, nursing a steaming mug of black tea while thirty students worked in huddled silence.

Near the back row, Lukas stared at the graph paper spread across his desk. 

It was the MCU Legacy Benchmark sheet Elena had given him on the fire escape. The third problem was an intimidating monster: calculating transient voltage spikes across an inductive coil during sudden circuit breaks. Elena’s original pencil notes in the margin had attacked the problem through an exhaustive three-page differential derivation. 

Lukas’s pencil hovered over the grid lines. 

He didn't know the full calculus integration formula Elena had used. Oakhaven High’s mathematics syllabus was two chapters behind the city schools, and the sophomore geometry class was currently plodding through basic conic sections. 

Instead, Lukas looked at the circuit diagram as a physical object. 

He thought about the way current coiled through copper wire—the physical pressure of electrons fighting the back-electromotive force, like water hitting a closed brass valve in Michael’s basement plumbing. If the magnetic field collapsed, the energy couldn't just vanish into the ether; it had to discharge into heat or arc across the gap. 

He scribbled down a three-line energy-conservation ratio, bypassing the differential equations entirely and calculating the inductive surge directly from magnetic field flux. 

A pale hand reached across the aisle and tapped a manicured fingernail against the corner of his desk. 

Lukas looked up. 

Elena was sitting diagonally in front of him, her navy coat draped over the back of her chair. She had her chin resting in one palm, her dark eyes looking down at his paper with quiet, intense scrutiny. 

"You didn't integrate over the time step," Elena whispered, her voice barely louder than the hum of the overhead fluorescent tubes. 

"I didn't need to," Lukas whispered back, keeping his eyes on Mr. Harris at the front desk. "If you know the inductance and the steady-state amperage, the energy stored in the magnetic field is just half of L times I-squared. When the switch opens, all that energy has to discharge through the circuit resistance in a fraction of a second, spiking the peak voltage. It’s a closed hydraulic balance."

Elena stared at his three lines of arithmetic for five long seconds. Her brow furrowed faintly, then relaxed into an expression of quiet, reluctant admiration. 

"You solved a third-order differential equation by treating electricity like a leaky garden hose," she murmured. 

"Hey, if the water doesn't spill, the math works," Lukas smiled faintly. 

"The engineering board graders in Meridian are going to hate you," Elena said, the faintest ghost of a smirk touching her lips. "They prefer suffering through five pages of Leibniz notation. But your final value is within two percent of the analytical solution."

She turned back to her own notebook, adding quietly under her breath: "Don't let Harris see that. He still thinks transistors are a passing fad."

Lukas folded the graph paper and tucked it carefully into his canvas binder. For the rest of the period, the dry, hollow feeling behind his sternum didn't feel quite as cold.

Serving Days Six and Seven of detention was less about punishment and more about learning how to outlast the school's maintenance department.

By Friday afternoon, Mr. Henderson had grown so accustomed to Lukas and Clara’s presence that he simply handed Clara the brass ring of master keys at three-thirty. 

"Gym basement," Mr. Henderson grunted, not even looking up from his crossword puzzle. "The folding bleacher chairs for the autumn band concert are covered in sawdust from the woodshop upstairs. Dust them, stack them in racks of twenty, and lock the double doors when you're done. If I smell cigarette smoke down there, both of you are doing roadwork for the county road crew."

"We don't smoke, Mr. Henderson," Clara said sweetly. 

"Good. Matches cost money," he replied, turning a page. 

The gym basement was a cavernous, echoing concrete bunker underneath the basketball court. The ceiling was crisscrossed with massive asbestos-wrapped steam mains that hissed and popped in the dim light of bare sixty-watt bulbs. 

For an hour and a half, Lukas and Clara worked with dry rags and push brooms, their boots squeaking against the painted concrete. 

"Seven days down," Clara announced, tossing her dust rag onto the last stacked rack of metal folding chairs with a metallic *clatter*. She leaned back against a concrete pillar, blowing a stray strand of dark hair out of her eyes. "Only three left next week. Monday, Tuesday, Wednesday. By Thursday, we are officially free citizens of the county."

"Assuming you don't bring any more alien flora into sixth-period biology," Lukas said, leaning on his broom handle. 

"That was a scientific specimen," Clara retorted, crossing her arms with a grin. "And for your information, that purple root had remarkable cellular resilience. Normal plant tissue would have frozen solid in Tuesday’s cold snap, but that thing was practically generating its own micro-climate inside the thermos before it woke up."

She looked at Lukas, her teasing expression softening into something more serious. 

"Have you looked at that gray stone in the shed lately?" she asked quietly, glancing toward the basement stairwell to make sure they were alone. 

"No," Lukas said honestly. "It’s buried under three coils of heavy alternator wire in the scrap bin. Cold as a brick. It hasn't buzzed or glowed since the day I touched it."

"Good," Clara said, nodding once. "Keep it that way. Julian said there was a municipal water truck parked by the edge of the woods near the crater on Wednesday. Only it didn't have town plates, and the guys in the cab were wearing navy parkas with some corporate logo on the breast pocket."

Lukas felt a small, prickling chill at the back of his neck that had nothing to do with the drafty basement. "The same people from the black van?"

"Julian couldn't get close enough to read the logo without looking suspicious," Clara said, picking up her backpack. "He said they had a core drill mounted on a flatbed. They're taking soil samples from the impact trench."

She stepped over and punched Lukas lightly on the shoulder. "So like I said: no glowing, no sparks, and no weird hero stunts. We get through next week, we take Elena’s practice tests, and we get out of this valley."

Lukas looked at his sister’s determined, sharp eyes and nodded. "Deal."

Friday evening arrived in Oakhaven with the smell of sweet birch smoke drifting from every chimney in the valley. 

Because the hard frost had set in early, the town’s customary *Frost Vigil*—the informal harvest tradition where kids carved lanterns to mark the end of the agricultural season—was in full swing across the neighborhoods. 

At the Oakhaven Children’s Home, the front porch was an absolute disaster zone. 

Newspapers were spread across the weathered floorboards, covered in pale yellow curls of turnip shavings and thick beet rinds. Unlike the glossy orange pumpkins people saw in magazines from the southern provinces, Oakhaven kids carved whatever winter root vegetables the local farmers sold cheap by the wooden bushel: dense, knobby yellow rutabagas, hard sugar beets, and massive winter turnips with purple shoulders.

Leo and Toby were sitting cross-legged on the porch floor, their tongues poked out in fierce concentration, hacking away at their root vegetables with dull kitchen butter knives and spoons. 

"Mine’s going to be a mountain goblin," Toby announced, holding up a mangled rutabaga with two crooked triangular holes for eyes. "With giant fangs that eat homework."

"Yours looks like a squashed potato," eight-year-old Leo shot back, carefully scooping out the pale, fibrous flesh of his sugar beet with an iron soup spoon. "Mine’s a gargoyle. Mr. Mike said gargoyles scare off bad luck."

Six-year-old Maya was sitting on a wooden milk crate beside them, wrapped in a bright red woolen scarf that swallowed half her face. She held a small, round turnip that Michael had already hollowed out for her, using the tip of a spoon to press a crooked, smiling mouth into the rind. 

"Lukas!" Maya cheered as Lukas walked around the corner of the house carrying a heavy galvanized tub filled with split firewood. "Look! My turnip is happy!"

"It’s very happy, Maya," Lukas smiled, setting the tub down on the porch gravel with a heavy, satisfying crunch. His arms were corded with muscle from hauling oak logs from the woodlot, his breath rising in white plumes against the twilight. "Much friendlier than Toby’s goblin."

"Hey!" Toby protested, though he immediately went back to sawing at his turnip’s jagged fangs.

The screen door squeaked open, and Michael stepped out onto the porch, carrying an old brass grease-gun and a tin of tallow candle stubs. He wore his heavy faded blue denim chore coat, lined with sheep's wool that had seen twenty winters. 

"Don't gouge your fingers with those spoons, boys," Michael warned gently, leaning down to inspect Leo's gargoyle. "Mrs. Gable is already busy enough inside without having to bandage four thumbs before dinner."

"Mr. Mike, is the boiler going to hold up this weekend?" Lukas asked, wiping sweat from his forehead despite the freezing air. 

Michael straightened up, wiping his thumbs on a rag. His weathered face broke into a quiet, proud smile. "Held all through the night at eleven pounds, Luke. That new brass valve seated perfectly. The hot water pipes on the second floor are actually warm for once. The girls' dorm didn't even need their second quilts."

He looked at the woodpile Lukas had been stacking along the stone foundation wall. 

"Good stacking," Michael added, nodding toward the neat cords of split oak. "Bark up, split side down so the rain doesn't rot the grain. You’ve got a good eye for balance, Lukas. That’s machinist work."

"Learned from the best," Lukas said. 

Before Michael could reply, the crunch of boots on the frozen gravel driveway signaled a visitor. 

Devan walked up the lane, wrapped in a thick, dark charcoal woolen coat with a crimson muffler tucked neatly around his collar. Under his arm, he carried a heavy brown burlap sack that crunched softly. 

"Evening, Mr. Mike," Devan called out respectfully, offering Michael a polite nod. "My dad sent me over with the winter rock salt for your front steps. Ten-pound bag of coarse grain from the rail depot."

"Tell your father I owe him a tin of pipe tobacco, Devan," Michael said warmly, taking the heavy sack. "Set your coat by the stove. Mrs. Gable has a kettle of cider simmering."

"Thanks, Mr. Mike. I can only stay a few minutes—Dad wants me back to help close up the hardware aisles," Devan said. He turned to Lukas, grinning as he looked down at the carpet of turnip shavings. "I see the goblin workshop is in full production."

"They're taking their civic duties very seriously," Lukas chuckled. 

Devan reached into his coat pocket and pulled out a small, twisted paper cone, pushing it into Lukas’s hand. The paper was warm and slightly greasy. 

"What's this?" Lukas asked. 

"Salted roasted chickpeas and crushed cumin," Devan said quietly, leaning against the porch rail. "Made them in the iron skillet before I walked over. Eat them while they're hot. Good for circulation."

Lukas popped a few into his mouth. They were smoky, savory, and packed with an earthy, fiery crunch that instantly warmed the back of his throat. "Man, your family really knows how to cook."

"Simple food for cold nights," Devan smiled. He glanced at Lukas's posture—the way his shoulders hung relaxed and low, his breathing steady and slow despite the freezing evening air. "You look good, Luke. The chest isn't locking up on you?"

"Not since Wednesday," Lukas said quietly, dropping his voice so the kids couldn't hear. "Out on the fire escape during detention, I practiced what you showed me. Just taking the breath all the way down, letting the natural warmth spread into the hands. It actually worked, Devan. My fingers stopped being numb."

Devan nodded slowly, his eyes showing a spark of quiet satisfaction. "That’s the first gate. *Sthira-sukha*—steadiness and ease. Most people fight their own bodies when they get cold or tired, tensing their shoulders and starving their blood of oxygen. Once you learn to let the breath move without force, your body takes care of the rest."

He clapped Lukas on the shoulder, his grip solid and reassuring. "Keep practicing it while you stack the rest of that wood. Work and breath go together."

"Lukas! Devan! Look!" Maya’s excited voice broke through their conversation. 

Michael had knelt down on the wooden floorboards, striking a long kitchen match. He touched the flame to the tallow wicks inside the hollowed-out turnips, then gently set the carved tops back on like jagged little hats. 

A warm, flickering amber light spilled out through the carved eyes and mouths, illuminating the porch in a soft, dancing glow. 

The knobby, imperfect vegetables were transformed. Maya’s smiling turnip glowed with a cheerful, hearth-like innocence; Leo’s gargoyle cast long, jagged shadows against the porch siding; and Toby’s jagged-toothed goblin looked ready to take a bite out of the twilight. 

Inside the house, the front door opened, letting out a rich, savory wave of slow-simmered beef broth, rosemary, and freshly baked dark sourdough. 

"Supper!" Mrs. Gable’s booming voice summoned them from the kitchen. "Wash those sticky turnip hands, and wipe your boots on the coir mat! If I find sawdust in the stew, you're all eating plain hardtack!"

"Stew!" Leo and Toby shrieked in unison, dropping their utensils and scrambling toward the open door. 

Devan chuckled, turning to jog back down the gravel driveway. "See you Monday, Luke. Keep your breath steady."

"See you Monday, Devan," Lukas waved. 

Michael gathered the shavings into an empty bushel basket, then paused, resting a hand on Lukas’s shoulder as they stood together on the quiet porch. 

The sky above was turning a deep, velvety indigo. A few sharp, cold stars were already burning through the twilight above the black pines of the ridge. The three turnip lanterns flickered faithfully on the porch rail, small islands of warm light holding back the dark mountain winter. 

Lukas took a slow, deep breath, pulling the icy pine air deep down into the center of his chest. 

There was no sudden jolt. No static shock. Just a clean, steady pulse of natural warmth flowing through his veins, grounding him to the solid oak planks beneath his boots and the noisy, warm house at his back. 

He didn't know what was buried in those woods, or who was drilling in the crater. But standing on the porch of the only home he had ever known, Lukas Vance knew one thing for certain:

He wasn't fighting the cold anymore. He was learning how to live in it.
