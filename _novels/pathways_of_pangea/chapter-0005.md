# Chapter 5: Archimedes and the Alien Rock

The shed behind the Oakhaven Orphanage wasn't much to look at from the outside. It was a weather-beaten structure of peeling white paint and rusted corrugated iron, originally meant for lawnmowers and rakes. 

But over the last three years, Lukas had painstakingly converted it into his sanctuary. The walls were lined with pegboards holding scavenged tools, spools of copper wire, and soldering irons. Stacks of old ham radios and gutted CRT televisions sat in the corners. Above a battered wooden workbench, a constellation map was pinned next to hand-drawn schematics of rudimentary motherboards. It smelled faintly of sawdust, ozone, and motor oil. 

Lukas stepped inside, his boots thudding against the plywood floor. He didn't bother turning on the overhead fluorescent bulb, opting instead for the small desk lamp clamped to his workbench. 

He unshouldered his backpack and let it drop onto the sturdy wooden bench. It landed with a heavy, uncompromising *thunk* that made the wood groan. 

"Close the door, keep the latch completely thrown," Devan’s voice came from outside. A second later, Devan slipped inside, closely followed by Clara and Julian. 

Devan instantly slid the heavy iron bolt into place, locking them in. He peeked through the dusty, spider-webbed window facing the street. "No black vans. No drones. We’re clear for now."

Julian pulled his hood down, his eyes darting around the cramped space. He always looked a little nervous in Lukas's shed, usually because he was afraid of accidentally leaning on an exposed wire. "An active network sweep," Julian muttered, rubbing his forehead. "Lukas, do you know how expensive a portable IMSI catcher that can bypass localized school bandwidth is? Whoever was in that van isn't local law enforcement. That’s black-budget stuff."

"I know," Lukas said quietly. He walked around the bench, unzipping the main compartment of his backpack. "Elena said it had a massive density profile. That’s why their radar picked it up."

He reached in with both hands, braced his core, and hoisted the grey stone out of the bag. He set it down in the center of the workbench. 

Silence fell over the shed. 

Julian leaned in, pushing his glasses up his nose. He squinted at it, his face falling in deep disappointment. "That’s it? We're dodging military-grade tech and intercepting localized network sweeps for a chunk of concrete?" 

"It's not concrete, Julian," Clara said, her voice unusually tight. 

She walked over, gently nudging Julian out of the way. She placed her plastic pencil case down on the bench right next to the rock. She clicked open the latch, but she didn't open it all the way yet. 

"Lukas, tell him what the drone said in the woods," Clara prompted. 

Lukas kept his eyes on the grey rock. He could still feel the phantom echo of it—that cooling, rushing energy that had slammed into his veins the moment he touched it. He felt incredibly alert, his thoughts firing at a thousand miles an hour, his earlier exhaustion a distant memory. 

"The drone said, *‘Energy signature has dropped to zero,’*" Lukas explained softly. He looked up at Julian. "Because before I touched it, this thing was glowing. Bright blue. Like bioluminescence, but... deeper. When I touched it, all that light, all that heat... it shot up my arm." 

Lukas looked at his hand. He clenched it into a fist, feeling a strange, solid density in his own muscles that hadn't been there yesterday. "I absorbed whatever was inside it."

Julian stared at Lukas, waiting for the punchline. When none came, he looked at Devan. Devan just nodded slowly. 

"Okay. Sure," Julian said, his voice creeping up half an octave. "My best friend is a human battery. Cool. Cool. Very normal Tuesday." He pointed at Clara’s pencil case. "And what did you steal? A glowing stick?"

"Worse," Clara murmured. 

She popped the lid off the pencil case. 

Even in the dim yellow light of the desk lamp, the object inside immediately commanded the room. It was a single, thick root, maybe four inches long. But it was entirely, violently purple. 

It wasn't just purple; it seemed to be producing its own faint, ambient light. As they watched, the root visibly twitched. Tiny, hair-like tendrils on the surface of the root were curling and uncurling, scraping uselessly against the cheap plastic of the pencil box. 

"Dude..." Devan whispered, leaning in closer. "Is that a bug or a plant?"

"It's a root from the weeds touching the crater," Clara said, pulling a magnifying glass from one of Lukas’s tool drawers. "I’ve been running the biology through my head all afternoon, and it’s mathematically impossible."

She held the magnifying glass over the root. 

"If a plant is bioluminescent on Earth, it’s usually due to a chemical reaction—luciferin reacting with oxygen. But this? The light isn't chemical. It’s pulsating to a rhythm. Like a cardiovascular system." She pointed to the curling tendrils. "And locomotion? Plants have motor cells that can react to stimuli, like Venus flytraps, but they rely on turgor pressure—water shifting inside the cells. Once plucked, it should die immediately. It’s been three hours, and it’s still moving on its own."

Clara looked up, her usually calm, pragmatic eyes wide with absolute fascination. "This completely violates everything we know about cellular biology on Pangea. It's synthesizing an energy source that isn't water or sunlight."

"Just keep it in the box," Julian took a small step back. "If an alien spore burrows into my brain, I'm haunting both of you."

Clara rolled her eyes, but carefully closed the plastic lid. "I need soil and a glass terrarium to observe it properly. I don't want it dying."

"Let's focus on the big problem," Devan said, shifting his weight. He pointed to the grey rock. "Elena told you it had a massive density profile. Why did a chunk of grey rock weigh so much that it flagged a ground-penetrating radar?"

Lukas turned to his workstation. "Let's find out." 

He was in his element now. Facing the impossible was terrifying, but facing a measurable problem? That was engineering. 

He pulled an electronic digital scale from his drawer—the one he used to measure precisely weighted counter-balances for his robotics projects. He set it on the table and zeroed it out. 

"Grab the rock," Lukas told Devan. 

Devan reached over, his biceps flexing visibly under his shirt as he picked the object up. "Man, you weren't kidding. It feels like a solid kettlebell." 

He placed it on the digital scale. The red LED numbers climbed rapidly before settling. 

"Twelve kilograms," Lukas read aloud. 

Julian did the mental math. "That’s over twenty-six pounds. For something the size of a grapefruit?" 

"Right," Lukas grabbed a notepad and a pencil. "Weight is twelve kilos. But we need its exact volume to figure out the density."

"Archimedes principle," Clara nodded. She grabbed a clean, plastic five-gallon bucket from the corner of the shed, while Devan fetched a watering can from outside to fill it up to the very brim. They set the water-filled bucket inside a larger plastic storage bin on the floor. 

Lukas carefully lifted the heavy rock. "Ready?" 

He lowered the rock into the bucket. As it sank entirely below the surface, the displaced water spilled over the brim, splashing down into the dry storage bin beneath it. Lukas pulled the rock back out, set it on a towel, and then carefully lifted the heavy bucket away. 

What was left in the plastic bin was the exact volume of water the rock had displaced. 

They poured that spilled water into a graduated measuring cylinder. 

"Roughly eight hundred and fifty cubic centimeters," Lukas read the side of the cylinder. 

He grabbed his pencil, the tip furiously scratching against the notepad as he plugged the numbers into the density formula: Density equals mass divided by volume (D = m/v). 

"Twelve thousand grams divided by eight hundred and fifty cubic centimeters..." Lukas muttered. He stared at the resulting number, his pencil stopping completely. 

"Well?" Devan asked, hovering over his shoulder. 

"Fourteen point one grams per cubic centimeter," Lukas said, his voice flat. 

Julian pulled out his phone. "Putting that in context... standard concrete has a density of around two point four. Solid steel is seven point eight." He looked up, his face pale. "Lukas... this thing is twice as dense as solid steel. The only things on this planet with that kind of density are heavy, toxic metals like Mercury, Uranium, or solid Lead."

Clara looked at the dull, grey stone, suddenly taking a step back. "Lukas, is it radioactive?"

"No," Elena’s voice echoed in Lukas's memory. *‘Massive density profile.’* He shook his head. "If it was throwing off uranium-level radiation, we would be severely sick right now. And it wouldn't have been glowing blue." 

He reached out and placed his hand flat against the cold surface of the stone. 

Nothing. 

The roaring, rushing energy he had felt in the woods was completely gone. But the phantom pulse deep in his chest—the new rhythm of his body that had absorbed it—thrummed happily in response to the touch. 

"It's not radioactive. It's a battery," Lukas said, a startling realization dawning on him. He looked at Clara’s moving, purple root, and then at the dense, heavy stone. "The pulse. The energy. Do you guys remember the static on the radios last night at midnight?"

"The network interference? Yeah," Julian said. "Completely crashed the gaming servers for three minutes."

"Whatever caused that interference... it dropped these things," Lukas theorized, his analytical mind stringing the chaotic events together into a line of logic. "And whatever organization was in that black van today? They know about it. They were actively scanning the terrain, looking for anomalies." 

"They were looking for this," Devan gestured to the table. "They want the battery, and they want the crazy purple weed. Which means, as of right now, we are in possession of extremely illegal, un-cataloged terrestrial objects." 

The reality of the situation crashed over the small shed. 

They were fifteen years old. They had homework. They had tests on the Industrial Revolution. They were supposed to be worrying about getting into Meridian Central University. 

Instead, they were standing in a poorly lit shed, hiding stolen materials from a highly funded, shadowy organization. 

"We bury them," Julian said immediately, rubbing the back of his neck. "Tonight. Under the cover of darkness. Put the rock in a trash bag, plant the purple thing deep in the woods, and we wipe our hands of it. We pretend it never happened."

Clara crossed her arms. "And give up a literal scientific breakthrough? If I can sequence the cells of that root, it could change bio-botany forever! It could cure diseases, Julian! I'm not burying it."

"She's right," Lukas said softly. 

He clenched his fist again. The incredible, icy fuel humming through his veins was a profound, life-altering sensation. For a kid who had felt sluggish and weak his whole life, dealing with chronic aches and pains he could never explain, today was the first day his body felt truly... *alive*. He didn't want this feeling to end. 

More importantly, a deeply ingrained, almost instinctual part of him told him that if he completely drained this new energy, he would regress right back to the fatigue. He needed more of it. 

"We don't bury it," Lukas said, looking his friends in the eyes. "We secure it. Clara, take one of my airtight lead-lined battery boxes from the corner to keep your plant in. The lead will shield any biological emissions from scanners." 

Clara nodded, a fiercely determined look on her face. 

"What about the rock?" Devan asked. "They scanned the school for it."

"They were looking for an active energy signature. But I drained it. Now, it's just heavy steel masquerading as stone," Lukas patted the grey rock. "I'll keep it down here in my heavy-metals crate. They won't spot it if they scan the yard. The orphanage is filled with old scrap iron and car engines anyway; the density profile will just blend into the noise."

Julian sighed deeply, dropping his head into his hands. "I am going to get arrested. My GPA is going to be ruined by a black-site prison record."

"Nobody is getting arrested," Devan clapped Julian on the back, nearly knocking the smaller boy forward. "We just keep our mouths shut, play it cool, and pretend we're perfectly normal students."

"Devan is right," Lukas said, trying to sound more confident than he felt. "For now, we act normal. Tomorrow at school, no whispering. No looking out the windows for vans. And whatever you do, don't mention this to anyone. Especially Elena."

"The scary astrophysics girl?" Devan raised an eyebrow. "Why? Is she a spy?"

"No," Lukas replied, grabbing the heavy stone to move it into his scrap crate. "But she's way too smart for her own good. If we slip up, she’s going to put the pieces together." 

Lukas locked the crate with a heavy brass padlock. As the latch clicked shut, the low, steady thrumming beneath his sternum beat out a powerful rhythm. The die had been cast. There was no going back to normal, not for any of them. The mundane world of Oakhaven was nothing but a fragile glass sheet, and the four of them had just stepped right on the crack.
