# Chapter 10: Solvents and Solder

Friday lunch at Oakhaven High was an assault on the senses. The cafeteria was a cavernous hall of ringing metal trays, screeching plastic chairs, and the unmistakable aroma of deep-fried frozen corn dogs competing with floor wax. 

At their usual table near the recycling bins, Julian looked like a man who had received tragic news from a doctor. 

He had his forehead pressed flat against the wood-laminate tabletop, both arms dangling limp at his sides. Between his elbows sat an uneaten carton of chocolate milk and a brown paper bag. 

"It’s dead," Julian groaned into the formica. His voice was muffled, trembling with theatrical despair. "Three years of faithful service, and it choked on a dust bunny. Just gave out with a high-pitched beep and a smell like burning hair."

Devan peeled a small, sour tangerine with infuriating dexterity, dropping the rinds onto a napkin. "What died, Jules? Your social life? Because that expired back in middle school."

Julian slowly rolled his head to the side, glaring at Devan through one fogged spectacle lens. "My graphics card, you uncultured Neanderthal. My desktop's primary GPU. The cooling fan seized at two in the morning during a twenty-man dungeon siege. The screen went magenta, and then... darkness." He let out a tragic sigh. "The fall guild raid is tomorrow night. If I can't log on, they’re replacing me with a twelve-year-old from Meridian City who plays on a fiber-optic connection."

"Can't you just buy another one?" Clara asked, carefully slicing the crust off a turkey sandwich using a plastic cafeteria knife. 

Julian let out a hollow laugh. "Buy another one? Clara, do you know what a mid-tier transistor board costs on a suburban allowance? I would have to sell a kidney. Or worse, get an actual part-time job at the bowling alley." He turned his tragic gaze toward Lukas. "Lukas. You're the junk whisperer. You have to resurrect it. I have five dollars and a bag of sour gummies with your name on them."

Lukas smiled faintly around a mouthful of tater tots. His body still felt stiff and heavy from Wednesday's sudden energy crash, but forty-eight hours of Mrs. Gable’s cooking and Devan’s breathing exercises had chased away the worst of the shaking. 

"Bring it to the shed tomorrow morning," Lukas said. "It’s probably just a blown electrolytic capacitor or a dried-out bearing in the fan. I have a box of spare fifty-volt components from an old television board. We'll test the leads."

Julian’s head snapped up, his pale face illuminated with pure reverence. "I take back every bad thing I ever said about your scrapyard hoard. You are a saint among men."

"He's an enabler," Clara corrected dryly, taking a bite of her sandwich. "You should let him read a book for once. His retinas are probably ninety percent blue light."

"Blue light builds character," Julian retorted, finally sitting up to puncture his chocolate milk carton. 

Devan chuckled, tossing a tangerine slice into his mouth. Then, his eyes drifted toward the far side of the cafeteria, near the large row of vending machines. His eyebrows twitched upward, and a slow, wicked grin spread across his face. 

He leaned across the table, nudging Lukas’s shoulder with an elbow. 

"Hey, sparky," Devan murmured in a low sing-song voice. "Don't look now, but your terrifying study partner is calculating your trajectory again."

Lukas stiffened. "What?"

"Table six," Devan whispered, nodding toward the quiet corner by the fire exit. "The ice queen."

Against his better judgment, Lukas glanced over. 

Sitting alone at a four-person table was Elena. While the rest of the cafeteria was a sea of wrinkled hoodies, spilled soda, and chaotic teenage noise, Elena sat with perfect, military posture. She was eating an apple with a fork and knife off a napkin, a thick, spiral-bound astrophysics textbook propped open against a stainless steel water bottle. 

As if sensing the gaze, Elena looked up. 

Her dark, sharp eyes cut straight across the noisy expanse of the room, locking directly onto Lukas. She didn't smile. She didn't wave. She simply held his gaze for two agonizing seconds, gave a microscopic, almost imperceptible nod of acknowledgment, and went right back to reading her chapter on orbital mechanics. 

Lukas felt his ears burn bright red. He violently turned his attention back to his lunch tray, nearly knocking his plastic fork to the floor. 

Devan dissolved into breathless snickering, burying his face in his hands. "Look at him! He's glowing again, but this time it’s not electricity!"

"Shut up, Devan," Lukas hissed, staring intensely at a glob of yellow mustard. "She was just looking up from her book."

"She nodded at you," Julian pointed out, adjusting his glasses with sudden, analytical suspicion. "A solemn, respectful nod. Like two rival generals meeting before a cavalry charge. Lukas, what did you promise her yesterday under the bleachers? Did you sign a contract in blood?"

"I didn't promise her anything," Lukas muttered, his face still uncomfortably warm. "She just wanted to make sure I was doing the calculus homework."

Clara rolled her eyes, shaking her head at all three of them. "You boys are pathetic. She’s just a girl with a library card and a neat sweater. Now finish eating. We have thirty minutes of freedom left before we have to report to the salt mines."

***

Detention on Friday afternoon took a turn for the profoundly unglamorous. 

When Lukas and Clara arrived at the biology lab at 3:30 PM, Mr. Avery wasn't there. Instead, leaning against the teacher's desk with his arms crossed over a grease-stained janitorial jumpsuit, was Mr. Henderson. 

Mr. Henderson had been the head night custodian at Oakhaven High for twenty-five years. He had knees that popped like firecrackers when he walked, a thick mustache stained faintly yellow from tobacco, and an absolute, zero-tolerance policy for student nonsense. 

"Avery had a faculty budget meeting," Henderson announced, his voice sounding like two cinderblocks grinding together. He reached into a gray plastic mop bucket on the floor and pulled out two dull, chipped metal putty knives. He tossed one to Lukas and one to Clara. 

They caught them awkwardly. 

"What are these for, Mr. Henderson?" Clara asked, eyeing the rusted metal blade with deep suspicion. 

"Library annex," Henderson grunted, picking up a five-gallon bucket and a roll of industrial brown paper towels. "The high-top study carrels and the underside of the wooden tables. Twenty years of accumulated spearmint, peppermint, and bubblegum, fossilized onto the varnished oak. Principal Harrison says you two like chemistry experiments. Today, your chemistry experiment is elbow grease."

Lukas looked at the putty knife. "Every table?"

"Every single one," Henderson said without pity. "Scrape it clean, bag the scrapings in the bucket, and wipe the wood down with mineral spirits. I’ll be back at five to inspect. If I run my hand under a bench and feel a single sticky bump, you're doing the cafeteria bleachers on Monday."

He turned and shuffled out the door, his boots squeaking against the linoleum. 

Ten minutes later, Lukas and Clara were on their hands and knees in the dusty, quiet alcove of the school library's rear annex. 

The task was horrific. 

"This is biological warfare," Clara gagged, leaning under a heavy oak desk with a flashlight in one hand and the dull putty knife in the other. "This gum isn't even pink anymore, Lukas. It’s black. It has petrified into sedimentary rock. A whole civilization of bacteria has evolved, built monuments, and gone extinct under this table."

*Scraaaape.* 

Lukas shoved his putty knife against a rock-hard lump stuck near the table’s crossbeam. The metal blade slipped, jarring his wrist, and shaved off a tiny, chalky sliver that smelled faintly of twenty-year-old cinnamon. 

"My wrist is going to snap," Lukas grunted, shaking out his fingers. His muscles still lacked their usual stamina, and the repetitive scraping was murder on his stiff joints. "We have eight tables left. We’re never going to finish by five."

Clara sat back on her heels, wiping sweat from her forehead with the back of her sleeve, leaving a streak of grey dust across her temple. She stared at the putty knife in her hand, her eyes narrowing into that fierce, stubborn squint she got whenever a plant sample refused to root. 

"No," Clara said firmly. "I refuse to spend my Friday afternoon chipping ancient mouth-tar with a butter knife. This is an engineering failure."

Lukas looked over at her, amused. "What are you going to do, Clara? Ask the gum nicely to leave?"

"I am a bio-chemist," she stated with immense dignity, getting to her feet. "Gum is composed of synthetic rubber elastomers, paraffin wax, and corn syrup polymers. It’s hydrophobic, which is why water doesn't touch it. But it is non-polar. And non-polar substances dissolve in non-polar solvents."

Lukas blinked. "Where are you going to get a non-polar solvent in the high school library?"

"Cover for me," she whispered. 

She darted out of the library annex before Lukas could stop her. 

Six minutes later, Clara returned, clutching a plastic cafeteria tray holding an absurd assortment of stolen contraband: three yellow squeeze-bottles of yellow French’s mustard, half a bottle of white vinegar from the salad bar, a small squeeze-tube of mineral-based hand lotion from the teacher's lounge, and an amber glass bottle of pure isopropyl alcohol from the first-aid kit in the nurse's station. 

Lukas stared at the haul. "Clara... what are you doing?"

"Science," she grinned wickedly. 

She pulled an empty paper coffee cup from her bag and began aggressively mixing the ingredients with the handle of her putty knife. The resulting concoction was a pale, foul-smelling yellow sludge that smelled powerfully of sour pickles, rubbing alcohol, and cheap lavender perfume. 

"Dip your rag in that," Clara ordered, handing him the cup. "Smear it on the gum. Wait sixty seconds for the acid and alcohol to break down the elastomeric bonds."

Lukas looked at the yellow sludge with extreme skepticism. "If Henderson smells this, he’s going to make us scrape the football stadium."

"Just test it!" 

Lukas sighed, took a rag, scooped a glob of the pungent yellow paste, and smeared it generously over a massive, petrified lump of black gum under the center table. 

They crouched together, watching the gunk. 

For forty-five seconds, nothing happened. Then, the black edges of the hardened gum began to soften, bubbling faintly as the vinegar and alcohol penetrated the dried sugar matrix. 

Lukas reached in with the putty knife and gave a gentle push. 

*Shhhk.* 

The entire lump peeled away in one smooth, effortless curl, sliding off the varnished oak like softened butter and landing with a wet *plop* onto the paper towel. 

The wood underneath was completely spotless. 

Clara let out a triumphant squeak, pumping both fists into the air. "Ha! Suck it, Henderson! Organic chemistry wins again!"

Lukas burst out laughing, leaning back against the library bookshelf. "You just invented weaponized pickle juice, Clara. It smells like a sandwich shop exploded in here."

"I don't care if it smells like a dumpster behind a deli," she chuckled, frantically scooping more sludge onto her rag. "It works! Slather the rest of the tables!"

For the next twenty minutes, they worked like a well-oiled machine. Instead of agonizing, wrist-straining scraping, they smeared Clara’s miracle solvent across the underside of the desks, letting the chemical reaction do the heavy lifting before wiping the softened sludge away in clean, satisfying strips. 

By 4:45 PM, every single wooden table in the annex was completely stripped of gum. 

The only problem was the atmosphere. The small, enclosed library annex smelled so intensely of sour vinegar, rubbing alcohol, and fake mustard that Lukas’s eyes were actively watering. 

"Open the bottom window," Clara wheezed, waving a notebook desperately through the air. "Fan it out! If Henderson walks in here, he’s going to think we were pickling a dead raccoon!"

Lukas shoved the lower sash of the heavy library window upward, letting the crisp, freezing autumn breeze rush into the room, clearing the pickle fumes. 

He leaned his forearms on the sill, taking a deep breath of fresh air, and looked down at the concrete loading dock in the alleyway two floors below. 

Parked beside the industrial dumpsters was a vehicle. 

Lukas’s breath caught in his throat. 

It wasn't a sleek, windowless black surveillance van. It wasn't a military vehicle. 

It was a battered, slightly rusted white flatbed truck with faded green municipal county decals on the door: *Tri-County Industrial Waste Management*. A stout driver in thick work boots and a high-vis orange vest was standing at the rear liftgate, leaning with a clipboard against the fender. 

The heavy metal basement double-doors of the school swung open. 

Stepping out onto the concrete ramp was Mr. Avery, accompanied by the school's junior custodian. Avery was carrying a bright red, heavy-duty plastic hazardous waste bin, sealed tightly with two thick yellow security zip-ties. 

Through the clear view of the second-floor window, Lukas could see through the translucent red plastic. Inside the bin, packed securely in bubble wrap, was the heavy glass specimen jar. The shriveled, dormant grey root was still nestled inside, cold and motionless. 

Mr. Avery set the red bin on the truck's hydraulic liftgate. The county driver scanned the barcode on the side with a clunky handheld terminal, stamped Avery's paper manifest, and hit the hydraulic lever. 

The metal lift groaned, hoisting the red bin into the cavernous, steel-walled bed of the truck among drums of used motor oil and spent school fluorescent bulbs. The driver slammed the roll-up rear door shut, clicked the heavy padlocks into place, and climbed into the cab. 

The diesel engine rumbled to life with a cough of grey exhaust, and the white truck slowly backed out of the school alleyway, turning onto the public county road and heading west toward the regional treatment facility. 

Clara walked up beside Lukas, leaning on the windowsill, her eyes tracking the retreating vehicle until it disappeared behind the maple trees. 

She stood quietly for a long moment, the cool wind blowing loose strands of brown hair across her cheeks. 

"It's really gone," she whispered. Her voice carried a complex, bittersweet tangle of emotions—a deep scientific grief for an impossible organism she would never get to study, mixed with a profound, bone-deep relief. 

Lukas reached over, lightly bumping his shoulder against hers. "It's better this way, Clara. We weren't ready for it."

"I know," she sighed softly, turning away from the window and looking at her dirty putty knife. "A high school biology lab wasn't the place. But... it was amazing, wasn't it? Even if it tried to eat my thermos."

"It was terrifying," Lukas corrected with a faint, tired smile. "And if you ever make weaponized pickle juice again, you're doing the breathing exercises with Devan."

Clara laughed, tossing a balled-up paper towel at his head. "Deal."

***

Saturday morning at the Oakhaven Orphanage arrived under a pale, crystal-clear blue sky. 

The first frost of the season had touched the grass overnight, dusting the lawn with sparkling white crystals that crunched beneath Lukas’s boots as he walked across the yard to his shed. The mountain air was sharp, smelling of pine smoke from Michael’s living room chimney. 

Lukas unlocked the shed padlock, pulled the door open, and breathed in the comforting, familiar scents of sawdust, engine oil, and cold iron. 

He didn't touch the heavy scrap crate in the corner. He didn't need to. He knew the dense grey stone was resting safely beneath the rusted alternators, completely dormant. 

Instead, Lukas set to work on his workbench. He clicked on the warm gooseneck lamp, plugged in his vintage soldering iron, and began laying out his tools: a spool of thin rosin-core solder, needle-nose pliers, and his digital multimeter. 

Around 9:30 AM, the gravel driveway crunched under running feet. 

The shed door flew open, and Julian shuffled in, looking like a man who had survived a harrowing winter crossing. He was bundled in his oversized grey hoodie, wearing mittens with cut-off fingertips, and cradling a heavy, rectangular black computer tower against his chest with both arms as if it were a fragile infant. 

Jogging right behind him was Devan, holding a thermos of Mrs. Gable’s hot spiced cider and shaking his head in disbelief. 

"He carried it two miles from his house," Devan announced, shutting the door behind them. "He refused to let me put it in my backpack. He claimed the vibrational frequency of my jogging would 'dislodge the optical drive.'"

"It’s an uncalibrated laser head, Devan!" Julian gasped, heaving the heavy steel tower onto the corner of Lukas’s workbench with a breathless groan. "If you drop it, the focal prism misaligns! It’s precision machinery, not a medicine ball!"

Lukas laughed, setting his pliers down. "Alright, let's see the patient."

Julian eagerly unscrewed the thumbscrews on the side panel, pulling away the black metal casing. Inside was a dense maze of ribbon cables, cooling fins, and circuit boards. In the center, slotted into the motherboard, was his graphics card. 

Lukas pulled the card out with practiced ease, laying the green fiberglass board flat on an anti-static rubber mat under the workbench lamp. He grabbed a magnifying glass and leaned in. 

"Here," Lukas pointed with the tip of an insulated screwdriver. "See that tiny black cylinder near the voltage regulator rail? The top is bulging."

Julian squinted through his glasses. "The top is supposed to be flat?"

"It's a ten-volt electrolytic capacitor," Lukas explained, reaching into a nearby tackle box of sorted electronics parts. "The cooling fan died, the heat spiked, and the electrolyte boiled inside the can. It popped the seal. Fifty cents worth of copper and aluminum foil."

Devan leaned over Lukas’s shoulder, watching with genuine curiosity. "Can you fix it?"

"Give me ten minutes," Lukas said. 

He grabbed the hot soldering iron in his right hand and a desoldering wick in his left. 

As the hot copper tip approached the delicate underside of the board, Lukas paused. He remembered Devan’s breathing instruction from yesterday. He dropped his breath low into his diaphragm, letting the tension leave his shoulders. 

He closed his eyes for a split second, feeling the quiet, empty channels in his forearms. 

When he opened his eyes, his hands were rock steady. 

There was no lightning. No blue glow. But his physical perception felt remarkably crisp. He could sense the exact boundary of the heat radiating from the iron’s tip, feeling the microscopic resistance of the solder alloy right as it reached its melting point. The old liquid metal liquefied cleanly, absorbed into the braided copper wick without scorching the surrounding fiberglass. 

*Click.* The ruined capacitor slipped out effortlessly. 

Lukas slotted a fresh, salvage-grade capacitor from his parts bin into the through-holes, snipped the excess leads with his wire cutters, and applied a tiny, silver bead of fresh solder. It set into a perfect, shiny cone. 

"Done," Lukas said, setting the iron safely into its metal holster. 

Julian stared at the board in awe. "That’s it? That took you three minutes!"

"The hard part is the fan," Lukas grinned, picking up an old twelve-volt cooling fan from a broken radio amplifier. "We have to splice the power leads to fit your three-pin connector. Grab the wire strippers, Jules. You're doing the twisting."

"I have delicate pianist hands, Lukas, I might give myself a blister," Julian complained, but he pulled off his mittens and grabbed the tool anyway. 

The shed door creaked open again. 

Clara walked in, carrying a small, weathered terracotta flowerpot filled with dark, damp potting soil. Growing in the center were three tiny, perfectly ordinary green peppermint sprouts, smelling clean and sharp in the chilly room. 

She walked over to the south-facing windowsill, setting the pot down where the pale morning sunlight streamed through the dusty glass. 

"What's that?" Devan asked, pouring a cup of hot cider from his thermos. 

"Peppermint," Clara said, brushing a speck of dirt from her sleeve. "Standard terrestrial Mentha piperita. It doesn't move, it doesn't chew metal, and it doesn't require a hazardous materials team. It just smells nice."

Devan smiled, handing her a plastic cup of cider. "A solid life choice."

Before Clara could answer, a loud, chaotic commotion erupted from the backyard. 

The screen door of the main house slammed open, and a pair of high-pitched battle cries echoed across the frozen lawn. 

"Charge! Protect the perimeter from the alien sludge!"

Tumbling into the shed with zero warning were seven-year-old Leo and Toby, both wearing bright red knit caps, waving plastic rulers and cardboard tubes like broadswords. Right behind them was eight-year-old Maya, wearing an oversized winter coat and holding a sketchbook open to a drawing of a multi-legged robot. 

"Lukas! Julian!" Leo shouted, his cheeks flushed bright red from the cold. "Mrs. Gable said Julian is a basement goblin who never sees the sun, so we're hunting him for extra cornbread points!"

Julian slowly lowered his wire strippers, staring at the twins in sheer horror. "A basement goblin? I am an esteemed guild strategist! I command armies!"

"Attack the goblin!" Toby yelled, lunging forward and poking Julian’s knee with a cardboard tube. 

"Ow! Devan, restrain your apprentices!" Julian yelped, scrambling behind Lukas's workbench for cover as the twins pursued him with ferocious giggles. 

Clara leaned against the workbench, laughing so hard she had to set her cider down, while Devan cheerfully cheered on the twins, critiquing their footwork. 

Lukas sat back on his wooden stool, the warm soldering iron resting safely beside him, the smell of peppermint and pine smoke drifting through the open doorway. 

His muscles still had a long way to heal. The mysterious, dangerous multiverse was still out there, spinning silently in the dark. And somewhere far to the west, the glittering towers of Meridian Central University were waiting for them. 

But right here, in a cluttered, drafty shed filled with noise, laughter, and the warmth of a noisy family, the world felt sturdy. The current was low, but the foundation was set. And for fifteen-year-old Lukas Vance, that was more than enough.
