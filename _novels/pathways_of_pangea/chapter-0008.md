# Chapter 8: The Depletion Rate

The administrative office of Oakhaven High School smelled of stale instant coffee, dry laser-printer toner, and the cheap imitation leather of the reception chairs. 

Through the venetian blinds, Lukas could see the wide expanse of the school’s athletic field. Outside, nearly eight hundred students and fifty teachers were standing around in loose clusters under the chilly autumn sky, waiting for the all-clear. Two bulky yellow fire engines were parked haphazardly on the access road by the front entrance, their red emergency lights silently flashing against the brick facade. 

Principal Harrison sat behind his faux-wood desk, rubbing his temples with both hands. He hadn't said a word for four agonizing minutes. He was just breathing through his nose, staring at the scarred, wooden desk surface like he was wishing a sinkhole would swallow the entire building. 

Beside Lukas, Clara was sitting upright, her hands clamped tightly in her lap. Her knuckles were stark white, and she had bitten her lower lip so hard there was a tiny bead of dried blood on the corner of her mouth. 

Mr. Avery stood near the door, shifting uncomfortably in his corduroy jacket. The heavy glass specimen jar rested in the dead center of Harrison’s blotter. Inside, the shriveled grey mass looked completely inert, like a bundle of tangled, dead brambles coated in dried mud. 

The heavy glass door clicked open, and Captain Miller, the senior battalion chief of the Oakhaven Volunteer Fire Department, stepped in. He had his heavy turnout coat unbuttoned, a handheld gas-detection monitor slung over his shoulder, and a grease-stained notepad in hand. 

"Well?" Principal Harrison asked, looking up with dark, exhausted circles under his eyes. "Do I have to call the county hazmat team, Miller? Am I shutting down the school board until Monday?"

Captain Miller pulled off his heavy gloves and wiped a sleeve across his forehead. "Save your budget, Harrison. We ran the four-gas sniffer over the entire third-floor science corridor. Zero volatile organics, zero carbon monoxide, zero combustible vapor. Whatever made that smoke or popped on the bench, it’s completely cleared out through the exhaust hoods."

Harrison exhaled a long, shuddering breath that sounded like a deflating tire. "And the specimen?" He gestured with a pen toward the jar on his desk. 

Miller leaned over, squinted through the thick glass, and tapped the side with a thick knuckle. The shriveled grey root didn't twitch. It didn't pulse. It lay entirely limp against the bottom. 

"We took a swab off the lid and ran a litmus strip," Miller said flatly. "Neutral pH. Non-caustic. Smells like burnt wiring and charred mulch. To be blunt, Bob, it looks like some kid took a bundle of dried wild grapevine, soaked it in motor oil and rubber cement, and tried to set off a homemade smoke trick in a soup bottle."

Lukas felt his lungs unlock, just a fraction. *It worked.* 

When that violent electrical shock inside him had collided with the plant, the force hadn't just stunned it—it had effectively starved the thing, forcing its biological functions into a deep, dormant coma. Without ambient metal or energy to feed on, it registered on standard emergency equipment as nothing more than inert, charred debris. 

"A smoke trick," Harrison muttered, his voice dropping into a dangerous, icy register. 

Captain Miller gave Lukas and Clara a long, stern look that carried thirty years of dealing with small-town teenage stupidity. "The fire engines are packing up. We’re logging this as an accidental false alarm due to an unauthorized student chemical reaction. But if my crew gets rolled out here again this semester because someone wanted to play junior chemist, I’m personally billing the school district for the diesel fuel."

"Understood, Miller," Harrison said through clenched teeth. "Thank you."

The fire captain tipped his helmet, pushed through the door, and left the room. 

As soon as the latch clicked shut, Harrison leaned forward, slamming both palms flat onto the desk. 

"Two weeks," Harrison hissed, glaring directly at Clara. "Two weeks of after-school detention. You will spend every afternoon scrubbing lab tables, peeling dried gum off the cafeteria benches, and dusting the bleachers. Both of you."

"Principal Harrison, Lukas wasn't even in the lab when it happened," Clara spoke up, her voice surprisingly steady despite the tremor in her hands. "He just heard the alarm and ran down the hall to help me when the bottle broke. It was my thermos."

"I don't care if he was on the roof with a bullhorn, Clara!" Harrison snapped, pointing a trembling finger at Lukas. "He was right in the middle of that room interfering with Mr. Avery! Michael has done enough for this town, taking in every stray kid without a word of complaint, and I shouldn't have to drag him down here to explain why children from the orphanage are setting off chemical hazards in my classrooms!"

Lukas swallowed hard. "We're sorry, Mr. Harrison. It won't happen again."

"You're damn right it won't," Harrison said, slumping back into his swivel chair and waving a dismissive hand toward the door. "Mr. Avery, take that jar down to the basement disposal closet. Lock it in the secondary hazardous cabinet until the district waste truck arrives on Friday. The rest of you, get out of my sight. Go join your homerooms on the field until the bell rings."

"Yes, sir," Lukas said. 

Clara stood up so fast her chair screeched against the linoleum. Lukas followed her out into the lobby, his hands buried deep in his pockets. 

As they pushed through the front double doors and stepped out into the chilly afternoon air, the crowd of students on the football field was already beginning to disperse. The emergency sirens had stopped, replaced by the low, idling rumble of the fire engines pulling away from the curb. 

Lukas took three steps down the concrete entrance stairs. 

And then, without warning, the floor dropped out from beneath him. 

It wasn't a physical stumble; it was an internal collapse. The roaring, high-octane furnace that had been humming behind his sternum since midnight suddenly sputtered. 

*Clank.* 

It felt as though an invisible breaker switch had tripped inside his chest. The warmth that had insulated his limbs from the autumn chill vanished in the span of a single heartbeat, replaced by an icy, bone-deep void. The faint blue lines that had been pulsing beneath the callouses of his hands faded completely into greyish pallor. 

Lukas gasped, his knees buckling. He caught himself against the cold steel handrail of the stairs, his vision blurring around the edges with dark, fuzzy static. 

"Lukas?" Clara turned, grabbing his elbow. "What's wrong? Are you okay?"

"I'm... fine," Lukas wheezed, but the words felt thick on his tongue. 

He wasn't fine. The energetic high that had carried him through the last thirty-six hours—the feeling of being plugged directly into a wall socket, the superhuman speed through the woods, the raw strength that had dented a steel gym door—was gone. Drained. Expended. 

He had absorbed a finite charge of energy from that stone in the crater, and he had spent it like water. Every careless display of power, every burst of adrenaline, every spark he had discharged into that vine had burned through the reserves. 

And now, the tank was completely empty. 

His muscles felt like wet sand. The chronic, dull ache in his lower back and knees—the familiar weakness that had plagued him his entire childhood on Pangea—came rushing back twice as hard, like a debt collector arriving to settle the bill. His palms trembled against the steel rail. 

"Your battery," Clara whispered, her analytical instincts cutting through the panic as she saw the color drain from his face. "It's empty."

"Yeah," Lukas breathed, forcing himself to stand upright, leaning heavily against the railing. "Dry."

"Come on," she murmured, looping her arm under his shoulder to bear some of his weight. "Devan and Julian are by the bleachers. Walk slowly."

They made their way across the damp grass of the football field. The student body was slowly filtering back toward the main building as the PA system crackled to life, announcing that sixth period was canceled and students should collect their belongings for dismissal. 

Huddled beneath the shadow of the home-team bleachers were Devan and Julian. 

Julian looked like he had aged five years. His oversized grey hood was pulled so low only his glasses and pale nose were visible. When he saw Lukas and Clara walking toward them, he let out a breath so loud it sounded like a puncture. 

"You're not in handcuffs," Julian muttered, shoving his hands into his pockets. "I was thirty seconds away from deleting my hard drive from my phone. I saw four fire trucks, two police cruisers, and a guy in a yellow hazmat apron."

"It was a volunteer firefighter with a gas sniffer, Julian," Devan corrected calmly, though the relief on his face was unmistakable. He took one look at Lukas's pale, hollowed cheeks and instantly stepped in, taking over for Clara and letting Lukas lean against his shoulder. "You look terrible, man. Did they interrogate you?"

"No," Lukas muttered, resting his forehead against the cold metal support beam of the bleachers. "Just detention. Two weeks."

"Two weeks?" Julian repeated, his voice cracking. "For almost blowing up the school with an extraterrestrial weed? You should be in a military tribunal!"

"Keep your voice down," a quiet, razor-sharp voice cut in from the shadow behind the bleachers. 

All four of them jumped. 

Elena stepped out from beneath the steel risers. She was holding her canvas tote bag over one shoulder, her white sweater immaculate, her dark bob completely undisturbed by the wind. She looked at Lukas’s trembling hands, then at Clara’s bloodied lip, before finally resting her gaze on Devan and Julian. 

"The fire department logged it as an expanding polymer," Elena said, her tone level and matter-of-fact. "Harrison bought it. Avery bought it. The incident report will be filed under standard lab negligence, which means local emergency dispatch won't flag it for county review."

Julian blinked behind his glasses. "Wait. You covered for them?"

"I gave him a simple explanation," Elena replied, shrugging slightly. "A high school biology teacher doesn't want to believe some mutant vine is trying to chew through his gas pipes. He'd much rather believe two idiot teenagers made a mess with a homemade chemistry prank. I just gave him an excuse he was willing to accept."

Devan squinted at her, his posture instinctively tightening, stepping half an inch in front of Lukas. "Why? You don't even know us."

Elena met Devan’s defensive gaze without flinching. "Because turning you in would mean the materials get confiscated by whoever was in that black van before anyone can actually study them. That would be a complete waste."

She turned her dark, perceptive eyes directly onto Lukas. 

"You look like you're about to pass out," she observed bluntly. "Earlier today in gym and the library, you looked completely wired—you dented a steel door with a basketball and made a heavy brass pen float in midair. Now your lips are pale, your hands can't stop shaking, and you look like a walking ghost." She tilted her head slightly. "Whatever charge you were running on... you burned right through it, didn't you?"

Lukas stared at her. He didn't have the energy to lie, and even if he did, she had watched him closely enough to connect every single dot. 

"Who are you going to tell?" Lukas asked, his voice rough. 

"No one," Elena said simply. 

Julian scoffed. "Just like that? Why should we trust you? For all we know, you're the one who called that black surveillance van to the gate yesterday!"

"If I had access to a black-budget surveillance van, I wouldn't be stuck in remedial Oakhaven," Elena retorted dryly. "I told you yesterday: I'm trying to get into the physics program at Meridian Central. I want to understand how things work. What Lukas did in that lab—and whatever that plant was doing to solid steel—makes zero sense according to normal physics."

She stepped forward, stopping two feet from Lukas. 

"I'm not going to snitch," Elena said, lowering her voice so only they could hear. "I just want in. When you test whatever is hidden in your shed... I want to be there. I want to see the observations. In exchange, I keep my mouth shut, I help keep Harrison off your backs, and I make sure you don't do something completely reckless like blowing up another circuit in front of a teacher."

The wind swept across the empty football field, rustling the dry leaves caught against the chain-link fence. 

Lukas looked at Clara. Clara offered a faint, reluctant nod; she knew as well as he did that Elena’s quick thinking was the only reason they weren't sitting in the back of a police cruiser right now. He looked at Devan, who gave a slow, pragmatic shrug. Julian just groaned, pulling his hood down further over his eyes. 

"Rule number one," Lukas said, his voice quiet but firm as he met Elena's gaze. "No more bringing anything to school. The stone stays locked in my shed. The plant is gone, locked in the school basement, and we leave it there. We don't touch anything until we know what we're dealing with."

"Agreed," Elena said without hesitation. "Bringing a live sample to school was an incredibly stupid move anyway."

Clara scowled at her, but didn't argue. 

"Rule number two," Lukas continued, shivering as a gust of autumn air bit through his denim jacket. "We act completely normal. We go to class. We do our detention. We don't look at black vans, we don't talk about strange energy, and we don't draw attention. We have to survive until graduation."

Elena looked at the four of them—the lanky, exhausted tinkerer, the blunt bio-nerd with dirt under her nails, the wiry Maluran martial artist, and the paranoid gamer shivering in a hoodie. 

"Fine by me," Elena nodded cleanly. "I'll see you tomorrow in Advanced Physics, Lukas. Try not to break anything on the way home."

She turned on her heel and walked toward the student parking lot, her movements crisp and deliberate, leaving the four of them standing alone under the bleachers. 

"Well," Julian muttered after a long pause. "She’s completely terrifying. I think I preferred the drone."

"She's smart," Devan said, adjusting his grip on Lukas's arm. "And she didn't sell us out. That counts for something in my book."

"Come on," Clara sighed, picking up her battered canvas bag from the grass. "The late bus is coming. Mr. Mike is going to kill us when he sees the detention slips."

***

The sun was sinking behind the western ridge of the Oakhaven mountains when Lukas finally dragged himself up the gravel driveway of the orphanage. The sky was bruised with deep shades of violet and burnt orange, the clear air dropping toward freezing as dusk settled over the suburban streets. 

The front porch light of the old house was already on. 

Standing on the porch, leaning with both forearms against the wooden railing, was Mr. Mike. He had his heavy wool flannel coat on against the evening chill, a mug of steaming black tea cradled in his hands. 

Before Lukas and Clara could even reach the wooden steps, the rusted screen door banged open. 

"They're back! They're back!" 

Tumbling out onto the porch in a flurry of untied sneakers and oversized sweaters were Leo and Toby, the energetic seven-year-old twins. They scrambled down the stairs, practically throwing themselves into Clara’s knees. 

"Did the high school really catch on fire?!" Toby asked, his eyes wide with excitement. "Timmy on the bus said the whole roof burned down and there were five fire trucks!"

"Did you fight a firefighter, Clara?" Leo chimed in, aggressively waving a plastic astronaut figure. "Did you set off a bomb?!"

"Nobody set off a bomb, you little monsters," Clara sighed, but the tension in her face melted as she instinctively reached down, ruffling both their mops of hair. "A thermos leaked on a hot plate, that's all. Now I have to spend two weeks cleaning chewing gum off the bleachers."

"Ewww!" Toby groaned in sympathetic horror. "Chewing gum is gross!"

Behind the twins, slipping quietly onto the porch, was Maya, an eight-year-old girl with dark pigtails and large, serious eyes. She didn't shout or ask about fire trucks. She walked carefully down the steps, holding a chipped plastic cup filled with cold tap water. She stopped in front of Lukas, looking up at his hollow cheeks and pale, trembling hands. 

"You look like you're about to fall over, Lukas," Maya murmured softly, extending the cup toward him. "Your hands are shaking."

Lukas felt an unexpected lump form in his throat. Even with every muscle in his back screaming in protest and his chest feeling like an empty cavern, he managed a faint, genuine smile. He took the cup and drank the water down in three large gulps. 

"Thanks, Maya," he whispered, gently tapping the top of her head with a knuckle. "Just tired. Had to do a lot of walking today."

"Alright, detective squad, that's enough interrogating," Mr. Mike’s warm, booming voice cut through the chatter. He stepped down to the bottom of the porch stairs, clapping a broad hand onto Toby's shoulder. "Back inside before your noses freeze off. Mrs. Gable put the beef stew on the table, and Toby, if I find your plastic astronauts on the staircase again, they're going straight into the scrap bin."

"Not Captain Astro!" Leo gasped, and the twins bolted back up the steps, shouting and laughing as they slammed the screen door shut behind them. Maya gave Lukas one last, watchful look before quietly following them inside. 

The front yard fell silent again, save for the dry rustle of the maple leaves blowing across the driveway. 

Mr. Mike turned his attention to Lukas and Clara. His weathered face was lined with quiet, heavy concern. His eyes lingered on Clara’s split lip, then shifted to Lukas. 

Lukas looked worse than he had in years. The false vigor of that strange, surging charge was completely stripped away, leaving his skin pale, his eyes hollow, and his posture slumped like an old man carrying wet laundry. 

"Harrison called," Mr. Mike said quietly. 

"Mike, it wasn't—" Clara started, her voice cracking with guilt. 

"I know," Mr. Mike cut her off gently. He reached out and placed a large, calloused hand on top of Clara’s head, smoothing down a stray tuft of her messy hair. "Harrison was screaming about liability and chemicals. But I know you two. You're reckless sometimes, but you're not vandals. Harrison said two weeks of detention."

He paused, looking directly into Lukas’s eyes. 

There was something in the older man’s gaze—a shadow of ancient, unspoken memory, an exhaustion that ran far deeper than a school disciplinary report. It was the same look he had carried on the roof at midnight, when he spoke of the heavy weather ten years ago. 

"You look worn out to the bone, kiddo," Mr. Mike said softly. "Go inside. Eat some hot stew. Get some sleep."

"Aren't you... angry?" Lukas asked, his throat dry. 

Mr. Mike looked out toward the darkened street, his jaw tightening slightly as if scanning the shadows beneath the suburban streetlights. 

"I'm not angry that you made a mistake," Mr. Mike murmured, turning back toward the front door. "I'm just glad you're both home in one piece. Oakhaven is a quiet town, Lukas. But quiet places have long shadows. Don't go looking for trouble where you don't need to find it."

He pushed the front door open, letting the warm, yellow light of the living room, the noisy chatter of twenty kids, and the rich scent of simmering beef stew spill out onto the porch. "Go on. The little ones saved you some cornbread."

Clara walked inside with a subdued nod, heading straight for the warmth of the kitchen. 

Lukas lingered on the porch for a moment. He turned, stepping off the wooden landing, and walked around the side of the house toward the darkened backyard. 

The shed stood in the deep twilight, peeling and silent beneath the pines. 

Lukas unlocked the heavy brass padlock on the door, slipped inside, and latched it behind him. He didn't turn on the workbench lamp. In the faint moonlight filtering through the dusty window, he walked over to the corner of the room, knelt down, and pulled open the heavy metal scrap crate. 

Sitting beneath a tangled mess of copper alternator coils and rusted iron car parts was the stone. 

It was dull, grey, and cold. Lukas reached out with a trembling hand, pressing his bare palm against the jagged surface. 

Nothing. 

No humming roar. No blue light beneath his skin. No rush of rocket fuel in his veins. Just cold, dead, impossible mass, sitting silent in the dark. 

Lukas sat back against the wooden wall of the shed, pulling his knees up to his chest, shivering as the cold of Pangea seeped into his bones. 

The storm had passed. The school was still standing. The mysterious watchers in the black van had lost their trail. But as he sat alone in the dark, feeling the vast, hollow emptiness echoing in his chest, Lukas knew the truth: 

Whatever strange power had awakened inside him... it was real. The universe outside their quiet world was alive, terrifying, and watching. And if he ever wanted to survive another pulse, he couldn't just be an amateur playing with live current in a shed. 

He had to learn how to endure the desert. 

He closed the crate, clicked the brass padlock shut, and walked back toward the warmth and lively chaos of the house. The long, quiet rhythm of his fifteenth year had finally begun.
