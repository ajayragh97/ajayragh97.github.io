# Chapter 9: Low Current

Thursday morning arrived not with a jolt of lightning, but with the slow, crushing weight of gravity. 

When the metal hammer of Lukas’s alarm clock clattered against its bells at 6:30 AM, his arm felt as though it had been cast in concrete. Hitting the shut-off latch required an actual, conscious exertion of willpower. He lay flat on his mattress, staring at the water-stained plaster of his ceiling, listening to the bitter autumn wind rattle the drafty windowpanes. 

Yesterday morning, he had sprung out of bed before sunrise, practically humming with restless vitality. Today, his joints felt stiff, his neck ached from sleeping crooked, and a deep, hollow fatigue weighed down his bones like soaked wool. 

The battery was dead. The high-octane charge he had absorbed from the crater stone was entirely gone. 

Yet, as he lay there breathing in the cold morning air, Lukas noticed something different. The chronic, dull ache that had plagued his childhood—the vague, sickly weakness he had never been able to put into words—felt altered. 

Before that strange jolt on his birthday, his body had felt sluggish and numb, like an ungrounded radio circuit choked with static. Now, even though he was completely drained of energy, the internal architecture remained. When he closed his eyes and focused, he could feel them: faint, hollow channels branching outward from beneath his sternum, running down his biceps, and tracing the bones of his forearms. 

They felt like dry riverbeds carved into stone. The water was gone, but the riverbeds were permanent. The pathways were real, and they were empty. 

Lukas forced himself to sit up, swinging his bare feet onto the cold wooden floorboards. He dragged on a faded flannel shirt over a thermal tee, laced up his work boots, and walked down the creaking staircase to the first floor. 

The lower level of the orphanage was already buzzing with its familiar, noisy rhythm. 

The dining room was filled with the rich, comforting scent of bubbling steel-cut oatmeal, toasted sourdough, and chicory-scented black coffee. Sitting at the long communal table were a dozen of the younger children, their chatter bouncing off the high, wood-paneled walls. 

Standing by the heavy six-burner cast-iron stove was Mrs. Gable. 

Mrs. Gable was a sturdy, silver-haired woman in her late fifties, with flour perpetually dusted across the bib of her denim apron and thick, practical spectacles perched on the tip of her nose. She had run the orphanage kitchen for as long as Lukas could remember, ruling the pantry with a wooden spoon and an iron-clad sense of order. 

"Don't you dare dip Captain Astro into your milk, Leo Vance," Mrs. Gable’s stern voice rang out over the clatter of silverware before Lukas had even cleared the doorway. She didn't even turn around from the griddle. "That plastic has been in the mud outside, and I won't have dirt floating in my dairy. Set him on your napkin."

Seven-year-old Leo pouted, reluctantly pulling the plastic astronaut back from his ceramic bowl, while his twin brother Toby giggled into his juice glass. 

"Morning, Mrs. Gable," Lukas murmured, stepping into the kitchen and reaching for a clean mug from the wooden drying rack. 

Mrs. Gable turned, using a metal spatula to slide a plate of scrambled eggs and warm toast onto the counter. Her sharp hazel eyes took one look at Lukas—at his pale face, the bruised circles under his eyes, and the slight slouch in his shoulders—and her stern expression softened into genuine grandmotherly concern. 

"Look at you," she sighed softly, shaking her head as she poured steaming black coffee into his mug. "Thin as a reed and pale as butcher paper. What did I tell you about staying up half the night tinkering with radios in that cold shed?"

"Just had trouble sleeping, Mrs. Gable," Lukas said, taking the mug with both hands, letting the ceramic warmth seep into his stiff knuckles. 

"Trouble sleeping, my foot. You take on too much," she scolded gently, pushing the plate of eggs toward him. "Eat every bite of that. And don't think I didn't hear about the fire alarms at the high school yesterday. Michael had to calm half the neighborhood down when the sirens went off. If you and Clara are getting yourselves into mischief, you come talk to me before Harrison gets his feathers ruffled."

"It was just an accident with a thermos, Mrs. Gable," Lukas said honestly. "Nothing dangerous."

"Mm. Well, eat up. You need meat on those bones."

At the far end of the dining table, eight-year-old Maya was quietly coloring a sketch of the Oakhaven mountain ridge in a spiral notebook. When Lukas walked over and sat down, she silently pushed a small glass jar of homemade blackberry jam across the wood toward his plate, offering a tiny, shy nod before returning to her drawing. 

"Thanks, Maya," Lukas smiled, spreading a dollop onto his crust. 

A moment later, Clara trudged down the stairs. She looked slightly more human than she had yesterday, having managed to wash the dirt from her hands, but her hair was hastily pinned back with a plastic clip, and she moved with the grim resignation of someone marching toward a firing squad. 

She sat opposite Lukas, pulling a bowl of oatmeal toward her without speaking. 

Michael walked in from the front hallway, holding a clipboard and his leather-bound ledger. He had traded his heavy flannel coat for a clean chambray work shirt, his grey-streaked beard neatly trimmed. 

"Morning, kids," Michael said, his deep voice naturally settling the chatter around the table. He set the clipboard down next to Clara and Lukas. "Harrison sent over the official detention notice by courier this morning. Three-thirty to five o'clock, every afternoon for ten school days. You're reporting to the maintenance supervisor and Mr. Avery."

Clara groaned softly into her spoon. "Ten days."

"Ten days," Michael confirmed, his tone firm, though there was no malice in his eyes. "You do the time, you do the work properly, and you apologize to Avery for ruining his table. And when your five o'clock bell rings, you come straight back here. The split oak by the woodpile needs stacking under the lean-to before the frost hits on Sunday."

"We'll get it done, Mike," Lukas said. 

Michael held Lukas’s gaze for a second, lingering on the hollow fatigue in his eyes, before offering a faint, approving nod. "Good. Eat your breakfast. The bus will be at the corner in ten minutes."

***

The morning air at the bus stop was biting, carrying the sharp scent of dead pine and damp earth from the mountains. 

Lukas stood with his hands buried in his jacket pockets, leaning his shoulder against the peeling paint of the wooden bus shelter. Every breath felt shallow, his chest tight and dry. 

Jogging up the sidewalk from the direction of the local grocery market was Devan. Unlike Lukas, Devan looked thoroughly refreshed, wearing a faded high school track jacket over his hoodie, his breath rising in steady, white plumes. Behind him, walking at a sluggish, miserable pace, was Julian, clutching a travel mug of tea and squinting against the morning glare. 

"You're alive," Julian greeted them, falling against the wooden bench. "I checked the regional news feeds four times before I went to bed. Not a single mention of high-tech drones, mysterious sinkholes, or shadow-ops black vans. Just an article about the middle school soccer team and a notice about road resurfacing on Route 9."

"Told you," Devan said, dropping his backpack onto the bench. He turned, looking closely at Lukas’s posture. Devan’s eyes narrowed slightly, taking in the way Lukas was favoring his left side and leaning heavily against the post. 

Without a word, Devan stepped up beside him. He reached out, his thumb and forefinger finding the tight muscle knot between Lukas’s neck and shoulder, pressing firmly with a precise, rotational pressure. 

Lukas winced, his shoulder dropping. "Ow. What are you doing?"

"Releasing the trapezius," Devan said calmly, adjusting his grip to press into the base of Lukas’s collarbone. "You're breathing through your throat, Lukas. Your shoulders are hunched up to your ears, your ribcage isn't expanding, and your blood oxygen is terrible. You look like you just survived a three-hour grappling tournament on an empty stomach."

"I just didn't sleep well," Lukas muttered, though the sharp pressure of Devan’s fingers was actually chasing away a throbbing headache he hadn't realized he had. 

"Don't fight the fatigue," Devan advised in a low voice, lowering his hands. He tapped the center of Lukas's chest, right over his sternum. "When my grandfather trained me in the yard back in Malura, he used to say that when the body is empty, you don't tense up to hold yourself together. You drop your weight down into the hips. Breathe low, into the belly, through the diaphragm. Let the exhale take twice as long as the inhale. It forces the blood to circulate without straining the pump."

Lukas blinked, looking at his friend. "Belly breathing?"

"Biomechanics," Devan shrugged casually, bouncing lightly on his heels. "Athletes do it to prevent lactic acid buildup. It resets the nervous system. Try it before you pass out in first period."

Lukas took a slow, deliberate breath, focusing on dropping the air past his chest down into his lower abdomen, exactly as Devan described. 

To his surprise, a faint, soothing wave of warmth drifted outward from his sternum. It wasn't the roaring blue fire of the stone, but a quiet, natural friction—a gentle reminder that his body’s new wiring was still there, responding to the rhythm of his breath. The tight knot in his lungs loosened slightly. 

"Thanks," Lukas murmured. 

"Don't mention it," Devan grinned. "Just don't collapse until after lunch. I need someone to copy chemistry homework from."

Before Julian could complain about his own nonexistent sleep schedule, the yellow school bus groaned around the corner, its brakes screeching to a halt against the curb. 

***

Walking through the double doors of Oakhaven High School felt remarkably, delightfully ordinary. 

The scorched ozone smell from yesterday had completely vanished, replaced once again by floor wax, cheap bleach, and the greasy steam of the cafeteria kitchen preparing mystery meat for lunch. There were no yellow hazmat ribbons blocking the science wing. There were no police cruisers in the visitor lot. 

The school had swallowed the incident whole, digesting it into the mundane machinery of high school gossip. As Lukas walked down the hall toward his locker, he overheard two freshmen arguing about whether Clara had accidentally thrown bleach into an open Bunsen burner or if Julian had tried to make homemade fireworks in a soup thermos. 

Lukas kept his head down, spun his locker combination, and grabbed his physics notebook. 

When he walked into Advanced Physics for second period, the lab room was half-empty. Mr. Harris was sitting at his cluttered front desk, his spectacles pushed down his nose as he red-penned a stack of quizzes. 

Lukas slid into his usual stool near the back. 

A moment later, the front door squeaked open, and Elena entered. 

She was dressed in a crisp navy-blue cardigan over a collared white shirt, carrying her meticulously organized black binder. She didn't look at the other students, and she didn't cast a dramatic glance around the room. She walked down the center aisle with her usual quiet, efficient grace and pulled out the wooden stool next to Lukas. 

She sat down, unzipped her pencil case, and placed a clean mechanical pencil on the black slate table. 

For three minutes, neither of them said a word. 

Then, as Mr. Harris stood up to write a series of vector equations on the whiteboard, Elena casually slid a folded sheet of lined notebook paper across the boundary between their desks. 

Lukas looked down. It wasn't a secret letter or a coded message. It was a perfectly transcribed copy of the advanced calculus notes Mr. Harris had delivered yesterday afternoon while Lukas was sitting in the principal’s office. 

In the upper-right margin, written in tiny, immaculate blue ink, was a single, brief notation:

*Gas lines inspected by municipal utility crew at 7:00 AM. System cleared. Black utility vehicle was not logged on the North-South municipal transit cameras this morning. Perimeter is cold.*

Lukas let out a quiet breath through his nose. He picked up his pencil, flipped the paper over, and scratched a quick line of text on the back before sliding it back across the slate:

*Thank you. For the notes. And for yesterday.*

Elena glanced down, read the words, and carefully folded the paper into her binder. She didn't smile, but the cool, distant edge in her dark eyes softened by a fraction of a millimeter. 

"Page fifty-eight," she whispered, keeping her eyes fixed on the board as Mr. Harris began lecturing on rotational inertia. "The problem set is due Monday. Focus on the conservation of angular momentum. It will help you understand how energy distributes across an uneven radius."

"Got it," Lukas murmured, opening his textbook. 

For the next fifty minutes, Lukas lost himself in the predictable, unyielding beauty of classical physics. Calculating mass, velocity, and frictional resistance felt like washing clean water over a bruised mind. On paper, every equation balanced. Every action had an equal and opposite reaction. Energy was never created from nothing, and it was never destroyed. 

He didn't know what plane of existence his body had originated from, or what kind of impossible laws governed the universe outside Pangea. But sitting in this dusty classroom with Elena scribbling formulas beside him, Lukas realized that if he ever wanted to master the strange current sleeping in his veins, he would have to learn the rules of this world first. 

***

At 3:30 PM, the final bell rang, echoing through the empty corridors like the closing of a cell door. 

While the rest of the student body stampeded out to the yellow buses and student parking lots, Lukas and Clara walked in the opposite direction, heading down the deserted corridor of the science wing. 

The biology lab was quiet. The afternoon sun slanted through the high, wire-reinforced windows, casting long, dusty shadows across the rows of black resin benches. 

Mr. Avery was sitting at his desk at the front of the room, wearing a beige cardigan with leather elbow patches, grading stacks of freshman biology tests under a gooseneck lamp. When the heavy wooden door creaked open, he looked up over his half-moon reading glasses. 

"Vance. Clara," Mr. Avery said, his voice flat, drained of yesterday’s fiery panic. He pointed with the eraser of his pencil toward the metal utility cart parked against the back wall. "Buckets. Rags. Spray bottles of industrial pine disinfectant. The front three rows of lab benches have wax and pencil lead ground into the slate. You scrub every square inch until the water runs clear. Then you empty the trash cans into the main dumpster by the loading dock."

"Yes, Mr. Avery," Clara said quietly. 

They set their backpacks by the door, grabbed two plastic buckets, and filled them with warm, sudsy water from the deep utility sink in the back corner. The sharp, astringent scent of pine oil and ammonia filled the room, overpowering the faint, lingering smell of scorched metal. 

For forty minutes, the only sound in the lab was the rhythmic scratch of Avery’s red pen, the ticking of the wall clock, and the wet slap of coarse rags against the resin tables. 

Lukas worked methodically. His arms still felt heavy and leaden, but as he leaned his weight into the sponge, scrubbing dried salt deposits off the black slate, he remembered Devan’s advice. He dropped his breath low into his diaphragm. He let his shoulders drop. 

Clara was scrubbing table four—the exact bench where the incident had occurred. 

She paused, wiping soapy water off her forearm with the back of her wrist. She glanced toward Mr. Avery, who was completely engrossed in correcting an essay on photosynthesis, and then signaled Lukas with a tiny flick of her chin. 

Lukas carried his bucket over, setting it down with a muted thud. 

"Look," Clara whispered, pointing her wet sponge at the iron gas valve bolted to the center of the bench. 

Lukas leaned in close. 

The heavy brass nozzle where the Bunsen burner tubing connected had been thoroughly scrubbed, but the metal itself was permanently altered. Right where the purple root’s tendrils had wrapped around the fitting, the smooth brass was etched with intricate, spider-webbing grooves. The metal looked pitted and corroded, as if it had been submerged in boiling hydrochloric acid. 

"It didn't just chew on it," Clara murmured, her voice barely a breath. "I examined the scissors this morning before I threw them in the scrap bin. The metal wasn't cut; the crystalline structure of the iron was enzymatically deconstructed. That plant was turning heavy terrestrial elements into cellular ATP."

Lukas looked at the scarred brass, feeling a shiver run down his spine. "Where is the jar?"

"Basement storage," Clara replied softly. "Locked in the yellow steel hazmat cabinet under the floorboards. Harrison told Avery it’s getting hauled away with the district chemical waste on Friday."

"Good," Lukas said firmly. "Let it go, Clara. We don't touch it."

Clara swallowed hard, her fingers tightening around her rag, but she nodded. "I know. It was stupid to bring it here. If you hadn't stopped it..." She looked up at him, her hazel eyes clouded with guilt. "Your hands, Lukas. When you grabbed it... it looked like an electrical arc."

"I don't know what it was," Lukas whispered honestly, keeping his eyes on the soap suds. "All I know is that whatever battery was inside me, it’s empty now. Completely drained."

He dipped his rag into the warm water, wringing it out until the soap foamed between his fingers. 

As he squeezed the coarse cloth, Lukas closed his eyes. 

He didn't try to force a burst of energy. He didn't try to smash a door or float a pen. He simply breathed—deep, slow, into his abdomen, exactly as Devan had shown him. He visualized the empty conduits in his arms. He thought of the copper traces on his motherboard schematics. 

*Low current,* he thought. *Just find the baseline.* 

For a long, quiet second, nothing happened. 

And then, deep beneath his breastbone, in the dead center of the hollow ache, a tiny sensation stirred. 

It wasn't the roaring, icy fire of the alien stone. It was microscopic—hardly a flicker, barely a fraction of a volt. A tiny, warm pulse of his own natural bio-electricity drifted outward, following the newly opened groove down his forearm, ending in a faint, prickling warmth across the skin of his palm. 

It lasted for half a second before dissolving back into his pulse. 

Lukas opened his eyes. He looked down at his wet, calloused hands. 

There was no blue glow. No smoke. No static shock. 

Just a quiet, solid confirmation. 

The stone had kickstarted the machinery, but the machinery belonged to him. The pathways were his own. And even here, in the cold, mana-less desert of Pangea, a trickle of current could still flow if he had the patience to nurture it. 

"Vance! Clara!" Mr. Avery’s sharp voice broke the silence. The biology teacher closed his ledger with a loud slap. "It’s five o'clock. Empty the buckets, wipe down the sink, and get out of here. I have faculty meetings to attend."

"Yes, sir," Lukas called back, dipping his rag one last time. 

Ten minutes later, Lukas and Clara pushed through the heavy exit doors of the high school, stepping out into the cool, purple twilight of late afternoon. 

The bus stop was empty, but walking down the sidewalk to meet them was Devan, a heavy canvas work jacket thrown over his track shirt, carrying three pairs of heavy leather work gloves in his hand. 

"Thought you might need a hand with the woodpile," Devan said with an easy grin, tossing a pair of gloves to Lukas and another to Clara. "Michael said if we get the two cords stacked before dinner, Mrs. Gable will bake an apple cobbler."

Clara laughed, the exhaustion finally lifting from her face. "Apple cobbler? You're on."

Lukas pulled the heavy leather gloves onto his hands. The chill of the mountain air bit at his cheeks, and his muscles still felt weary, but as the three of them walked down the suburban street beneath the glowing amber streetlights, the path ahead felt steady. 

The strange, impossible universe was still out there, waiting in the dark. But for now, in the quiet heart of Oakhaven, Lukas had firewood to stack, friends at his side, and a lifetime of physics left to learn.
