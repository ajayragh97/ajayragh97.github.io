# Chapter 11: PVC and Starlight

Sunday afternoon smelled of roasted cardamom, damp gravel, and sawdust. 

Oakhaven’s Main Street was a sleepy two-lane strip of red-brick storefronts, hardware shops, and quiet diners, bordered by towering sugar maples dropping the last of their golden leaves. On Sundays, half the shops had their blinds drawn, giving the town a slow, lazy heartbeat. 

Lukas walked alongside Devan, carrying a dented metal toolbox in one hand and a slip of paper with Michael’s handwriting in the other. 

"Three-quarter-inch brass pipe elbows, two rolls of Teflon tape, and a pack of rubber furnace gaskets," Lukas read aloud from the paper. "Michael said the basement boiler is wheezing like a dying accordion again, and if we don't patch the intake valve before next week's hard freeze, we're all sleeping in parkas."

"My dad’s shop has the brass fittings in the back aisle," Devan said, tossing a rusted iron bolt from hand to hand with rhythmic ease. "He buys wholesale surplus from the rail yards down in the valley. Way cheaper than the downtown hardware store."

They turned down Market Lane, stepping toward a modest storefront with green-painted trim and gold lettering on the glass: *Nair & Sons Provisions & Hardware*. 

The second the brass bell above the door jangled, a rich, intoxicating wave of scent hit Lukas: warm cloves, toasted mustard seeds, turmeric, dried chilies, and the sharp, clean metallic tang of new nails and copper wire. It was a bizarre, wonderfully comforting mix of a Maluran specialty grocer and an old-fashioned general supply shop. 

Behind the counter, stacking wooden crates of dried ginger, was Devan’s father. 

Mr. Nair was a broad-shouldered man with deep brown skin, wavy silver hair cropped short, and the exact same bright, quick smile as his son. He wore a faded plaid shirt with the sleeves rolled past his forearms, revealing thick, corded muscle built from thirty years of loading freight. 

"Look who finally decided to visit his old man," Mr. Nair boomed in a deep, melodic voice, wiping his hands on a clean cloth. "And he brought the engineer with him. Good afternoon, Lukas."

"Good afternoon, Mr. Nair," Lukas smiled, setting the toolbox on the floor. 

"Michael needs boiler parts again?" Mr. Nair guessed, chuckling as he leaned against the counter. "I told him five years ago that old furnace belongs in a museum. One of these days, that iron beast is going to retire itself."

"He's trying to keep it alive until spring," Lukas said, handing over the slip. 

"Of course he is. That man would fix a broken shoe with duct tape and stubbornness," Mr. Nair laughed warmly, scanning the list. "Aisle four, bottom shelf behind the zinc screws, Devan. Fetch the brass elbows. I’ll cut the gaskets."

As Devan jogged down the aisle, Mr. Nair reached under the glass display counter, pulled out a small brown paper bag, and pushed it across to Lukas. The bag was warm, smelling heavenly of fried dough and sweet coconut. 

"My wife made sweet *unni appam* this morning," Mr. Nair said softly, giving Lukas a knowing, fatherly look. "Devan told me you kids had a scare at school on Wednesday with some science project. You look a bit peaked, Lukas. Eat one while it's warm."

Lukas felt his cheeks warm, touched by the simple, unpretentious kindness. "Thank you, Mr. Nair. It was just a small mess with a thermos. Nothing serious."

"Good. School is for learning, not for catching fire," Mr. Nair said, winking as he began cutting rubber rings from a sheet on his workbench. "Devan talks about that university down in Meridian every single dinner. He says you're going to build robots while he figures out how to rebuild shattered knee joints."

"That's the plan, sir," Lukas said, taking a bite of the warm pastry. It was crispy on the edges, soft in the center, and tasted of caramelized brown sugar and cardamom. 

"Work hard for it," Mr. Nair told him, his tone warm but earnest. "Oakhaven is a good place to grow up. Quiet, safe. But the world outside is big, boys. If you get the chance to learn how it works, you take it with both hands."

"Yes, sir."

Five minutes later, armed with a brown paper bag of brass fittings and half a dozen sweet pastries, Lukas and Devan walked back into the crisp autumn sunlight. 

"Now," Lukas said, adjusting his grip on the toolbox, his eyes darting toward the north edge of town. "Miller's Scrapyard is only three blocks over."

Devan grinned, nudging Lukas’s shoulder. "Still trying to upgrade that PVC pipe you call a telescope?"

"It’s not just a PVC pipe, Devan. It has an achromatic doublet lens," Lukas defended with mock indignation. "I just need a brass sleeve to build a proper focusing mount so the eyepiece stops wobbling in the cold."

"Right, right. Precision junk," Devan laughed. "Lead the way, Captain Astronomy."

***

Monday afternoon arrived with the dull, gray monotony typical of late October. 

By the time the final school bell rang at 3:30 PM, the hallways were empty within minutes. Lukas and Clara reported directly to the second floor for detention. 

Today’s punishment was relatively humane compared to Friday’s gum-scraping ordeal. Mr. Avery, clearly feeling a twinge of guilt over the previous week's chaos, had assigned them to the empty chemistry prep room to organize glass test tubes, wash porcelain crucibles, and sort plastic beakers by volume. 

By 4:45 PM, they were finished. Avery signed their detention slips with a weary nod, reminding them they had seven days left, and dismissed them into the chilly twilight. 

Instead of heading down to the student bus loop, Lukas walked down the empty corridor toward the rear fire exit, carrying a long, padded olive-drab canvas bag slung over his back. 

Clara stopped at the doorway, raising an eyebrow at the bulky bag. "You actually brought it to school?"

"I left it in my locker all day," Lukas whispered defensively, patting the canvas strap. "Tonight is supposed to be the clearest night of the month. No clouds, zero humidity, and the new moon means minimal light pollution. I want to test the brass focusing mount I fitted yesterday."

"On the school roof?" Clara crossed her arms, shivering as the outside draft crept through the door seals. "Lukas, Harrison gave us detention for a leaky thermos. If he catches you trespassing on the roof access ladder, he will literally make us scrub the boiler room with toothbrushes."

"The roof ladder in the gym hallway is unlocked on Mondays for the HVAC maintenance crew," Lukas whispered, looking around the deserted hallway. "I’m just going up for twenty minutes while the twilight fades. The late bus doesn't leave until five-thirty anyway."

Clara sighed, rubbing her forehead, but she couldn't hide the faint smile tugging at her mouth. "You're an idiot, Lukas Vance. A stubborn, stargazing idiot."

"Wait for me by the bus loop?"

"Ten minutes," she warned, pointing a finger at him. "If you fall off that roof and break an arm, I'm telling Mr. Mike you tripped over an action figure."

Lukas grinned, turning and jogging lightly down the stairs toward the gym wing. 

The maintenance ladder was tucked behind a heavy metal fire door in the athletics corridor. Lukas hoisted his heavy canvas bag, reached up, and climbed the steel rungs one by one. He pushed open the heavy roof access hatch, feeling the cold, biting mountain breeze hit his face as he stepped out onto the wide, gravel-topped flat roof of Oakhaven High. 

The view took his breath away. 

Below him, the small town of Oakhaven was a quiet patchwork of red brick, asphalt, and glowing yellow porch lights nestled in the valley. Far to the south, the distant skyline of Meridian City was nothing more than a faint, purplish smudge of light on the horizon. 

Nestled in the shadow of the western mountain ridge at Oakhaven's northern latitude, late October twilights arrived fast and early. The sun had already dropped behind the granite peaks before five o'clock, plunging the valley into a crisp, violet dusk. It was still far too bright for faint nebulae, but for bright targets like Jupiter rising in the east, the twilight was perfect—the soft ambient background cut the blinding glare of the planet's disk without washing out its cloud bands. 

Lukas set his bag down on a wooden maintenance pallet near the parapet wall. 

He unzipped the canvas, pulling out his pride and joy. 

It was ridiculous to look at. The main tube was a four-foot length of heavy-duty gray plumbing PVC, held together with black electrical tape and fitted with a polished four-inch optical objective lens he had salvaged from a broken 1970s slide projector. But yesterday afternoon, using the brass fittings from Mr. Nair’s shop and parts from the scrapyard, he had constructed a smooth, helical focusing mount that held a clean glass eyepiece with zero wobble. 

He mounted the tube onto a rickety wooden surveyor's tripod, tightened the thumbscrews, and pointed the barrel toward the eastern sky, where the bright white pinpoint of Jupiter was rising above the pines. 

He leaned in, closing his left eye and pressing his right to the cold glass. 

He reached out, his calloused fingertips turning the newly fitted brass focusing ring with a slow, smooth rotation. 

The blurry white smear in the lens snapped into crystalline, astonishing focus. 

Jupiter wasn't just a dot. It was a pale, cream-colored disk, banded with faint amber stripes, flanked by four tiny, perfect diamonds of light—its Galilean moons, hanging suspended in the dark like beads on an invisible string. 

Lukas exhaled a soft breath that fogged the freezing air. 

"Achromatic doublet," a quiet, calm voice said from behind him. 

Lukas jumped so hard his hip bumped the tripod leg, sending Jupiter swinging wildly across the view. He spun around, his heart hammering against his ribs. 

Standing beside the open roof hatch, wrapped in a heavy charcoal-grey wool coat with a thick knitted scarf around her neck, was Elena. 

She had both hands tucked neatly into her coat pockets. She didn't look startled, and she didn't look like she was sneaking around. She looked as though she had merely taken a stroll onto the roof to enjoy the evening air. 

"How did you get up here?" Lukas breathed, trying to calm his racing pulse. 

"The access door was propped open with an empty tape roll," Elena said, stepping calmly across the gravel roof toward his pallet. "And I saw you carrying a four-foot canvas cylinder down the hallway after detention. Given your known behavioral tendencies, it didn't require advanced calculus to deduce where you were heading."

Lukas looked at his clunky telescope, suddenly feeling painfully self-conscious. Compared to her immaculate coat and clean leather boots, his homemade rig of PVC pipe, duct tape, and scavenged brass looked like a pile of literal trash. 

"It's... just something I put together from the scrapyard," Lukas mumbled, rubbing the back of his neck. "To look at the moons."

Elena stopped beside the tripod. She tilted her head, her dark eyes studying the telescope with intense, microscopic scrutiny. 

She didn't laugh. She didn't make a snide comment. 

Instead, she reached out with a gloved hand, lightly touching the brass fitting near the eyepiece. 

"A threaded plumbing adapter turned down on a lathe for a standard inch-and-a-quarter eyepiece," she observed softly. She stepped around the front, looking down at the objective bell. "And that front lens... that’s an achromatic doublet from an old projector assembly, isn't it?"

Lukas blinked, completely thrown off balance. "You... recognize it?"

"Of course I recognize it," Elena said, looking up at him, her breath rising in a pale wisp. For the first time since Lukas had met her, there was no icy distance in her expression. Her dark eyes were bright, illuminated by genuine, unguarded enthusiasm. "A cheap single lens gives you awful purple fringing around bright targets like Jupiter. To fix chromatic aberration with scrap parts, you'd have to hand-lap the seating ring and align the elements yourself. Did you grind the cell fit on your bench?"

"I... yeah," Lukas stammered, his ears turning red in the chill. "It took three months. I used jeweler's rouge and a piece of plate glass on my workbench."

Elena stared at him for a long moment, an expression on her face that was remarkably close to awe. 

"That is an absurd amount of manual labor for a four-inch aperture," she said softly. Then, she looked at the eyepiece. "Can I see?"

Lukas stepped aside immediately. "Yeah. Of course. It’s dialed in on Jupiter right now."

Elena leaned down, her scarf brushing against the PVC barrel. She placed her eye against the rubber cup of the eyepiece. 

For thirty seconds, she didn't move. She didn't say a word. The only sound on the roof was the low hum of the distant town and the whisper of the autumn wind through the parapet grates. 

"The seeing is incredible tonight," Elena murmured, her voice carrying a quiet reverence that made Lukas’s chest tighten. "The Great Red Spot is just coming over the eastern limb. And Callisto's shadow is cutting right across the cloud deck."

She slowly stood upright, looking at Lukas. Her cheeks were flushed pink from the cold, and her eyes held an unmistakable, infectious excitement. 

"You built an instrument with near-zero optical distortion using ten dollars worth of plumbing supplies and a discarded projector," she said, shaking her head faintly. "You really do want to go to Meridian Central, don't you?"

Lukas looked down at his calloused, wire-scarred fingertips, then out toward the distant, faint purple glow of the southern horizon. 

"More than anything," Lukas said honestly. "I don't have family money. I don't have parents with alumni connections. All I have is the stuff I can build with my hands and whatever scholarship tests I can pass. If I don't get into the engineering department at MCU... I'm just an orphan working a wrench in Oakhaven for the rest of my life."

Elena watched him quietly, the wind tugging loose strands of dark hair from her scarf. 

"My father is an associate dean of applied mathematics at Meridian Central," she said quietly. 

Lukas looked up, surprised. 

"He thinks I should study accounting or institutional finance," Elena continued, her voice tinged with a cold, quiet defiance. "He thinks astrophysics is a sentimental waste of a mathematical aptitude. He thinks looking at the stars doesn't generate quarterly dividends."

She reached out, her gloved fingers resting gently on the wooden tripod leg. 

"That’s why I’m in Oakhaven," she whispered, looking up at the burning stars. "He wanted me in a quiet prep environment with zero distractions to focus on business entrance exams. But I don't want his money, Lukas. And I don't want his career. I want to build radio telescopes. I want to know why the universe expands, and I want to know what exists in the empty spaces between the galaxies."

She looked back at him, offering a small, genuine smile—the first real smile Lukas had ever seen on her face. 

"You're going to pass those entrance exams, Lukas," Elena said firmly. "And when you do, you're going to build the receiver arrays for my research."

Lukas felt a warmth bloom in his chest that had nothing to do with batteries, currents, or alien stones. It was simple, human, and wonderfully terrifying. 

"Deal," Lukas managed to say, his throat feeling slightly dry. 

Below them, the loud, dual-tone horn of the late school bus blared from the parking lot loop, echoing off the gymnasium brick. 

"That’s the five-thirty bus," Lukas said, quickly checking his watch. "Clara is going to murder me."

Elena laughed—a clear, light sound that startled Lukas with its warmth. "Pack your telescope, Lukas Vance. We wouldn't want Miss Vance charging up here with a mop handle."

Working together, they loosened the thumbscrews, packed the brass mount into the padded canvas bag, and zipped it shut. 

As they walked side by side toward the roof hatch, leaving the cold starlight behind to rejoin the noisy, mundane world below, Lukas felt the quiet pathways inside his chest thrum with a gentle, steady ease. 

He didn't have all the answers. He didn't know what kind of surge had struck him on his birthday, why his body had reacted to that fallen stone, or who was driving the black vans in the shadows. But as he stepped down the ladder into the warm, yellow light of the hallway, the future didn't feel like a terrifying desert anymore. 

It just felt like an open road, waiting under the stars.
